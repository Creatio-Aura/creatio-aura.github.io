White vs pink vs brown noise: all three are a steady hiss made of every pitch at once. What changes is how much of the sound sits in the high pitches and how much in the low ones. Here's how each one sounds, what each covers, and a simple way to pick yours, on any white noise machine or app.

## What the colors mean

Noise is a mix of every pitch at once, from deep bass to high hiss. The color says how the loudness is shared out between the low and the high pitches. The names borrow from light: white light mixes every color evenly.

| Noise | From low to high pitches | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **White** | The same strength at every pitch | A hiss, like TV static or a fan on high |
| **Pink** | Gets weaker as the pitch rises (3 dB less for each octave up) | Steady rain, or wind in the trees |
| **Brown** | Gets weaker faster (6 dB less for each octave up) | A low rumble, like distant surf or a far-off waterfall |

**White noise** has the same strength at every single pitch. Our hearing works in octaves, and each octave up holds twice as many pitches as the one below. So white noise puts most of its energy in the highs, and we hear it as bright and hissy.

**Pink noise** evens that out: every octave carries the same energy. To most ears it sounds balanced, neither sharp nor booming.

**Brown noise** drops off faster still, so most of its energy sits in the bass. It's named after the botanist Robert Brown and the random "Brownian" motion he described, not after the color. You'll also see it called red noise.

You may come across grey, green or blue noise too. Grey noise is tuned so every pitch sounds about as loud as the others to human ears. "Green noise" has no technical meaning; apps use the name for noise that sits mostly in the middle pitches.

## How to choose

1. **Think about what you want to cover.** Noise covers a sound best when it shares its pitch. Voices, birds or a TV next door are higher sounds, so try white or pink. Traffic, a neighbor's bass or a furnace are low sounds, so try brown.
2. **Listen side by side.** Play each one for a minute at about the same volume, and keep the one you like.
3. **Set the volume yourself.** Use the lowest level that covers what you want covered, and keep a speaker away from your head.
4. **Mix them, if you like.** A low brown noise underneath with rain on top is a common mix. There are no rules.
5. **Choose how long it plays:** all night, or a timer that stops it or fades it out.

For a baby's room, keep the machine well away from the crib and the volume low.

## Questions people ask

### Is brown noise better than white noise?
Neither is better; they sound different. Brown has less hiss and more rumble, and covers low sounds like traffic well. White covers higher sounds like voices. Play both at about the same volume and keep the one you like.

### What's the difference between pink and brown noise?
Both get quieter as the pitch rises, but brown drops off twice as fast: 6 dB for each octave up against pink's 3 dB. So pink sounds like steady rain, and brown like a deeper rumble.

### What color noise is rain?
Steady rain sounds a lot like pink noise, which is why pink noise is so often described that way.

### Why is it called brown noise?
After the botanist Robert Brown and the random "Brownian" motion he described, not after the color.

### Can I play noise on my iPhone without an app?
Yes. Go to Settings > Accessibility > Audio & Visual > Background Sounds, turn on Background Sounds, then choose a sound. Apple lists ocean, rain and bright noise among them. You can also start it from Control Center, set a timer to stop it, and, since iOS 26, use its Equalizer to change the tone.

### Can I mix brown noise with rain or a fan?
Yes, with an app that plays more than one sound at a time. A low brown noise under rain is a common mix, and a recording of your own fan works too. To record yours, see [Your own fan, anywhere](/journal/record-your-fan-noise/).

### Can I put the noise behind me or to one side?
With headphones and an app that places sounds one by one, yes. See [Spatial audio sleep sounds](/journal/spatial-audio-sleep-sounds/) for how that works.

## Bottom line

White is a hiss, pink is steady rain and brown is a low rumble. Pick by what you want to cover, listen to each at about the same volume, keep the volume low, and mix them if you like.
